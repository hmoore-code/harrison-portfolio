## Project: Worker Feedback Club

- Filter: Building communication tools
- Title: Construction site feedback platform
- Organisation: Worker Feedback Club, with Willmott Dixon, Multiplex and Skanska

### The challenge

Big construction sites hold hundreds of workers from dozens of firms. Most stay quiet about unsafe or unfair conditions, afraid of being blacklisted. Major contractors pay for this silence in expensive rework (**up to £33m a year**) whilst workers pay in poor mental health. Eg, male construction labourers in England are 3 times more likely than average to die by suicide. [(source)](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/birthsdeathsandmarriages/deaths/articles/suicidebyoccupation/england2011to2015)

### What I built

I'd spent years as an electrician on sites like these, so I knew this silence first hand. In 2017, during my masters, I started Worker Feedback Club to give workers a safe way to speak up. My idea for a **Slack-style feedback tool** won a future of work competition launched by Tom Watson, then Labour's deputy leader. It also made me the first artist given a place in the Royal College of Art's business incubator.

![Harrison on a construction site](harrison-on-site.jpg)

My biggest lesson was about **positioning**. I originally pitched the platform as a way to "protect worker welfare", but contractors got defensive, and workers felt infantilised. So I reframed it as a "communication problem" that was costing everybody, and this gave both sides a goal they shared. The app became a tool for all. Managers posted updates. Workers reported problems. And anybody could thank a colleague for doing great work.

### The impact

The new framing won everyone over. Skanska, Multiplex and Willmott Dixon signed paid contracts. On Willmott Dixon sites, about **half the workforce adopted the app**, checking it on average **12 times a week**. Shared documents saved site teams around **2 hours a week**. And workers were finally heard on important issues. At Tesco Woolwich, supervisors used private reports to replenish drinking water and eyewash after earlier requests were ignored.

## Project: Tempr

- Filter: Building communication tools
- Title: Angry words app
- Organisation: Creative Thought Partner
- Year: 2026
- Role: Designer and builder
- Result: An AI-built chatbot that turns your angry words into calm requests using Marshall Rosenberg's Nonviolent Communication. People have used it to improve business relationships and friendships.
- Skills: Applied AI, Prompt engineering, Learning experience design

### The challenge

When we're angry or hurt, our words can push people away and impact our relationships at work and at home. We often don't realise that **beneath our anger is a request for an unmet need**.

### What I did

Psychologist Marshall B. Rosenberg's **Nonviolent Communication** framework helps people identify their needs and ask for them collaboratively. I was learning NVC myself in tandem with learning to build with AI, so in June 2026, I embedded NVC into a web app that's accessible to anybody with an internet connection.

[NVC graphic goes here]

My first version gave users multiple-choice questions, but it couldn't ask for the nuances or the facts behind the feelings. So I rebuilt it as a chatbot. Tempr can now **explore what happened**, **ask what you want**, and **offer you two nonviolent ways to ask for what you need**.

#### NVC graphic

- Title: 4-part NVC framework
- Line 1: When I see… [evidence]
- Line 2: I feel… [emotions]
- Line 3: because I need… [need]
- Line 4: Would you be willing to… [request]?

### What happened

Users valued Tempr's questions more than its answers. People found it useful to be walked through their own situation: from the feelings to the need to the request and to the resolution. **One user rekindled a friendship** he was going to let drift, saying he "definitely would not have done it without using Tempr."

### The skills I could bring to your team 🫵

I can turn expert methods into something a beginner can use, design for people to *learn by doing*, and write instructions that make an AI behave well. I can use these skills equally well in learning and education settings. For example, in:

- Helping learners ask for what they need
- Turning expert methods into guided practice
- Teaching emotional literacy
- Rapidly prototyping learning tools
- Improving the style and tone of learning products

## Project: Clever constraints for writers

- Filter: Helping people learn
- Title: Writer's prompt library
- Organisation: Creative Thought Partner
- Year: 2024 to 2025
- Role: Creator and host
- Result: A growing bank of creative, self-serve writing prompts for nonfiction writers, each with a mini-lesson, plus monthly challenges for my newsletter readers. Participants published over 50 pieces of writing.
- Skills: Learning experience design, Community engagement, Writing
- Card button: Try a prompt
- Evidence link text: Try the writing prompts
- Evidence link: https://harrisonmoore.notion.site/Writing-Prompts-from-Harrison-LIVE-75e0e11b460c435a9ea8553845744ca2
- Image: notion-prompts.jpg (your screenshot of the Notion prompt library)
- Image alt text: A grid of colourful writing prompt cards, such as Do nothing and Write for the dustbin
- Pop-up image: notion-prompts.jpg
- Pop-up image alt text: A grid of colourful writing prompt cards, such as Do nothing and Write for the dustbin

### The challenge

If you asked a hundred people what it takes to produce great writing, and enjoy it, I bet that none of them would say "constraints". But people freeze when given total freedom.

### What I did

In developing my own practice, I'd built a library of 29 writing prompts. In 2025, I turned the prompts into a series of challenges. Each one set a different focus and a different limit (for example, from 6 words to 900). I published everyone's responses, and we met on group calls to reflect.

We found that responses fell as the rules stacked up. 30 people published a 6-word story. 14 when it was exactly 49 words. And only 2 for a 900-word essay. I named the pattern the "constraint sweet spot" and published it for other writers to use.

### What happened

People are productive when constraints are tight but not too tight. Across 4 challenges, participants published 53 pieces, collectively drawing 2,677 views. One participant who hadn't written for over a year said the prompts "keep the spark alive". Several prompts also became core exercises in my Writing Workshop.

### The skills I could bring to your team 🫵
I treat limits as a design tool, then test them with real learners to find the level that gets people started.

## Project: Full Cycle Writing Workshop

- Filter: Helping people learn
- Title: Cohort-based writing workshop
- Organisation: Creative Thought Partner
- Year: 2024 to 2025
- Role: Designer and facilitator
- Result: A 6-week live online course for 30 working professionals across 5 time zones, taking them from idea to published piece. 8 writers published new work, and the group continued meeting after it ended.
- Skills: Curriculum design, Community building, Coaching and facilitation
- Card button: Learn more
- Evidence link text: Read my workshop write-up
- Evidence link: \[<https://www.thenewworkday.com/p/the-questions-every-writer-should-ask>\]
- Image: workshop-cohort.png (the cohort in a live session, Zoom names blurred)
- Image alt text: Zoom screenshot of the Full Cycle Writing Workshop cohort during a live session
- Pop-up image: workshop-cohort.png
- Pop-up image alt text: Zoom screenshot of the Full Cycle Writing Workshop cohort during a live session

### The challenge

When the online writing course Write of Passage closed, many alumni (including me!) were devastated to lose the community they wrote with. 37 writers signed up to my writing workshop. They had tonnes of interesting ideas but no repeatable process, and fear of publishing kept many stuck in a state of procrastination.

### What I did

I stepped in and designed and ran a live 6-week course that took writers through 6 distinct stages of a writing project: curating an information diet, research, thesis, style, editing, and finally publishing. To help lower the stakes, I instructed each writer to write their first draft as an email to one person they trusted.

After session 4, many writers said they were now struggling to choose between different drafts, so I adapted the editing session, using powerful questions to identify each piece's main point: Why am I writing this? What do I ultimately believe? Who is this going to be most valuable to?

### What happened

Answering these questions and writing to one trusted person got the majority of writers unstuck. Everyone produced drafts, and at least 8 published. When the course ended, the students asked to keep meeting every Sunday as a writing group, which they offered to co-host.

### The skills I could bring to your team 🫵
I design learning experiences that make people feel safe enough to try, then adapt them when learners get stuck.

### Try it yourself

- Button: Try a writing challenge
- Image: form-1-letter.jpg, form-2-listicle.jpg, form-3-dialogue.jpg (the original slides from session 4)

In the first half of my workshop, I steered writers towards working out what they wanted to say before worrying about structure or style. But form and substance are always tangled up. The shape you write in changes how you think, and it changes what ends up on the page.

So in session 4, I asked each writer to take the idea they were working on and run it through one of these 3 forms, all taken from a [Form Library](https://www.thenewworkday.com/p/the-shape-of-your-ideas-matters) I built for the course. Try it with an idea of your own. It will probably feel messy. That's fine: the point is to see what a new shape does to your idea.

#### Form 1: A handwritten letter

Write directly to one person. It could be someone you know, or a public figure you admire or disagree with. A personal letter creates intimacy, almost like a confession, so you can show raw thinking without worrying about sounding perfect.

- Handwrite it on a single A4 sheet, and fill no more than 1 page.
- Start with "Dear..."
- Tell them what you most want them to understand about your idea.
- Don't polish it. Let it be honest and a bit raw.

#### Form 2: A listicle

A listicle breaks your idea into separate chunks. It makes for a clear, quick read, and it's a good way to unpack an idea into its most interesting parts.

- Write exactly 7 items: questions, lessons or short stories.
- Give each one a clear, punchy heading.
- Keep each item to 1 to 3 sentences.
- If you're stuck, try "7 questions I'm exploring" or "7 lessons I've learned".

#### Form 3: A made-up dialogue

Write an imaginary conversation between 2 voices, both played by you. One might be wise or supportive, the other full of questions or doubts. They could be 2 friends, family members or colleagues, or 2 strangers you think should meet.

- Start with a thorny question, problem or opportunity.
- Let the voices go back and forth for 1 to 2 A4 pages.
- Look for the tensions and ideas that only come out in conversation.
- Don't force a conclusion. Follow where the conversation goes.

## Project: The Steel Core

- Filter: Designing compelling experiences
- Title: DocFest awards trophies
- Organisation: Sheffield Doc/Fest, UK's leading documentary film festival
- Year: 2017 to 2019
- Role: Designer and producer
- Result: The "Oscars" of the UK's biggest documentary film festival. I designed and produced trophies for award winners, three years running. My designs were featured in Screen Daily and It's Nice That.
- Skills: Creative production, Project management, Partnerships

- Pop-up image: steel-core/winners-2017.jpg
- Pop-up image alt text: Sheffield Doc/Fest's 2017 award winners in a group, holding their steel trophies

### The challenge

Sheffield Doc/Fest is the UK's leading documentary film festival. For its awards, it wanted a trophy good enough to earn a nickname, like the Oscar. And it had to send filmmakers from all over the world home with a piece of Sheffield, a city built on steel.

### What I did

My winning design, called the Steel Core, was a solid bar of Sheffield stainless steel, shaped like a relay baton for one generation to pass on to the next. I wanted it to be emblematic of the whole city, so I had a local steelworks cut the bars, I finished each of them by hand, and a Sheffield engraver added winners' names in a typeface from Stephenson & Blake, the city's foundry that was once the world's largest maker of metal type.

### What happened

It's Nice That reported the Grand Jury winner leaving "with the coveted Grand Jury award stuffed into his jacket pocket", which was indeed nice, as I'd designed the trophies to be easily carried. Doc/Fest contracted me to make trophies 3 years running, 33 awards in total, before Covid cancelled the awards ceremonies.

### The skills I could bring to your team 🫵
I turn a place and its story into an object people can hold, and manage production from concept to delivery.

## Project: Solo show

### The challenge

All artists hope their work will sell one day. In 2016, I needed mine to sell within weeks. I had a coveted place on a master's at the Royal College of Art, starting that September, but no way to pay for it as masters funding is limited.

### What I did

So I went home to Sheffield and gave myself 6 weeks to make and sell a solo show, alongside a crowdfunding campaign. I stopped strangers in the street, asked them about Sheffield's past, present and future, and turned their answers into original artworks.

Because the show had to sell, I made many pieces in batches, like the city's factories, with prices starting at £20, so anyone could take one home. I ran everything myself, from research to hanging the show, with backing from 4 local businesses.

### What happened

The pricing strategy paid off. In 6 weeks I made 63 works. 30 people came to the opening night, and the show sold about £3,000 of work. The crowdfunding campaign raised £1,800 more. Together, that money helped pay for my master's.

### The skills I could bring to your team 🫵
I can fund an idea by making something people want to support, then deliver it on a tight timeline and budget.
