### The challenge

Big construction sites hold hundreds of workers from dozens of firms. Most stay quiet about unsafe or unfair conditions, afraid of being blacklisted. Major contractors pay for this silence in expensive rework (**up to £33m a year**) whilst workers pay in poor mental health. Eg, male construction labourers in England are 3 times more likely than average to die by suicide. [(source)](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/birthsdeathsandmarriages/deaths/articles/suicidebyoccupation/england2011to2015)

### What I built

I'd spent years as an electrician on sites like these, so I knew this silence first hand. In 2017, during my masters, I started Worker Feedback Club to give workers a safe way to speak up. My idea for a **Slack-style feedback tool** won a future of work competition launched by Tom Watson, then Labour's deputy leader. It also made me the first artist given a place in the Royal College of Art's business incubator.

![Harrison on a construction site](harrison-on-site.jpg)

My biggest lesson was about **positioning**. I originally pitched the platform as a way to "protect worker welfare", but contractors got defensive, and workers felt infantilised. So I reframed it as a "communication problem" that was costing everybody, and this gave both sides a goal they shared. The app became a tool for all. Managers posted updates. Workers reported problems. And anybody could thank a colleague for doing great work.

![The Worker Feedback Club app on a phone, showing the Miner's Road site feed, with buttons to recognise great work or flag an issue, and workers giving each other credit for safety and quality](screen-site-feed.png){whole}

### The impact

The new framing won everyone over. Skanska, Multiplex and Willmott Dixon signed paid contracts. On Willmott Dixon sites, about **half the workforce adopted the app**, checking it on average **12 times a week**. Shared documents saved site teams around **2 hours a week**. And workers were finally heard on important issues. At Tesco Woolwich, supervisors used private reports to replenish drinking water and eyewash after earlier requests were ignored.

### The challenge

When we're angry or hurt, our words can push people away and impact our relationships at work and at home. We often don't realise that **beneath our anger is a request for an unmet need**.

### What I did

Psychologist Marshall B. Rosenberg's **Nonviolent Communication** framework helps people identify their needs and ask for them collaboratively. I was learning NVC myself in tandem with learning to build with AI, so in June 2026, I embedded NVC into a web app that's accessible to anybody with an internet connection.

[NVC graphic goes here]

My first version gave users multiple-choice questions, but it couldn't ask for the nuances or the facts behind the feelings. So I rebuilt it as a chatbot. Tempr can now **explore what happened**, **ask what you want**, and **offer you two nonviolent ways to ask for what you need**.

#### NVC graphic

- Title: 4-part NVC framework
- Line 1: When I see… [evidence]
- Line 2: I feel… [emotions]
- Line 3: because I need… [need]
- Line 4: Would you be willing to… [request]?

### The impact

Users valued Tempr's questions more than its answers. People found it useful to be walked through their own situation: from the feelings to the need to the request and to the resolution. **One user rekindled a friendship** he was going to let drift, saying he "definitely would not have done it without using Tempr."

### The skills I could bring to your team 🫵

I can turn expert methods into something a beginner can use, design for people to *learn by doing*, and write instructions that make an AI behave well. I can use these skills equally well in learning and education settings. For example, in:

- Helping learners ask for what they need
- Turning expert methods into guided practice
- Teaching emotional literacy
- Rapidly prototyping learning tools
- Improving the style and tone of learning products

## Project: Full Cycle Writing Workshop

- Filter: Helping people learn
- Title: Cohort-based writing workshop
- Organisation: Creative Thought Partner
- Year: 2024 to 2025
- Role: Designer and facilitator
- Result: A 6-week live online course for 30 working professionals across 5 time zones, taking them from idea to published piece. 8 writers published new work, and the group continued meeting after it ended.
- Skills: Curriculum design, Community building, Coaching and facilitation
- Card button: Learn more
- Evidence link text: Read my workshop write-up
- Evidence link: \[<https://www.thenewworkday.com/p/the-questions-every-writer-should-ask>\]
- Image: workshop-cohort.png (the cohort in a live session, Zoom names blurred)
- Image alt text: Zoom screenshot of the Full Cycle Writing Workshop cohort during a live session
- Pop-up image: full-cycle-writing.png
- Pop-up image alt text: The Full Cycle Writing wheel, with 6 stages: info diet, ideation, the what, the how, polishing and publishing

### The challenge

When the renowned online writing school **Write of Passage** closed, many alumni (including me!) were devastated to lose the community they wrote with. **37 writers**, looking for a new home, signed up to my writing workshop. They had fascinating ideas but no repeatable process, and fear of publishing kept many stuck in procrastination.

### What I did

I designed and ran a live 6-week course that took writers through **6 distinct stages** of a writing project: **curating an information diet**, **research**, **thesis**, **style**, **editing**, and **publishing**. To help lower the stakes, I instructed writers to write their first drafts as emails to one person they trusted.

![An email to Aunt Sarah that begins: I know this might seem strange, but I have been exploring the topic of X](aunt-sarah.png){16:9}

Many writers said they were struggling to choose between multiple ideas, so I adapted the sessions, incorporating **powerful questions** to help them pin down their main point:

- Why am I writing this?
- What do I ultimately believe about it?
- What do I want the reader to think, feel, or do differently?

Normally in life, function determines form. But with writing, you can invert that logic and **experiment with letting form determine function**. This was the writers' favourite session: putting their ideas through one of 3 forms: a personal letter, a listicle, or a fictitious two-way dialogue.

![Slide: Change the form, change the thinking. Write a letter, write a list, write an imaginary dialogue](dialogue.png){16:9}

### The impact

Expressing their ideas by email, questioning their motivations, and experimenting with forms got the majority of writers unstuck. Everybody produced drafts. At least **8 writers published new work**. And when the workshop ended, they asked to keep meeting every Sunday as a writing group, which they offered to co-host.

### The skills I could bring to your team 🫵

I can take skills people find overwhelming and break them into manageable parts, facilitate live learning environments, and change course quickly when students need it. I'd love to use these abilities to help more people learn and grow. For instance:

- Giving people permission to be bad at something
- Enhancing courses based on learner feedback
- Turning expert know-how into tools anyone can use
- Developing constraints and prompts to get people unstuck
- Running pilots, scaling courses and building communities

##### 📝 Personal Letter

Write directly to a specific individual, using a personal, confessional tone.

- Benefits: Good for building a sense of intimacy; easy to show vulnerability and raw thinking.
- Example: Rainer Maria Rilke's 'Letters to a Young Poet'
- Example link: https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/German/RilkeLetters.php

##### 🔢 Listicle (or Roundup)

Break down insights or ideas into a numbered or bulleted list.

- Benefits: Great for clarity and easy skimming; watch out for oversimplification.
- Example: This very post is a listicle! But here's Mari Andrew's: '100 things I know'
- Example link: https://mariandrew.substack.com/p/100-things-i-know

##### 🗣️ 2-Way Dialogue

Play both sides of a conversation between characters and allow the ideas to unfurl.

- Benefits: Communicates ideas in a relatable way through the voices of real (or imagined) people.
- Example: My piece 'Why Coffee is Nuts'
- Example link: https://www.thenewworkday.com/p/coffee-is-bad

##### 📧 Email Chain

Simulate or use an actual back-and-forth email exchange with another person.

- Benefits: Encourages concise writing; each exchange can add a fresh angle or clarifying point.
- Example: Elle Griffin's 'Letters to an Anarchist'
- Example link: https://www.elysian.press/p/the-letters-to-an-anarchist-post

##### 🎙️ Interview

- Benefits: Lets you probe questions systematically, revealing different facets of a topic.

- Example link: https://www.noahpinion.blog/p/interview-patrick-collison-co-founder

##### 📖 Story-Led

- Benefits: Engaging approach that ties personal or historical stories to bigger themes.

### The challenge

Sheffield DocFest is the UK's biggest documentary film festival. For its awards ceremony, it wanted a trophy good enough to earn a nickname, like the Oscar. And it had to send filmmakers from all over the world home with a piece of Sheffield, a city built on steel.

![A DocFest audience watching a film in a dark cinema](audience.jpg){16:9}

### What I did

My winning design, called the Steel Core, was a solid bar of Sheffield stainless steel, shaped like a relay baton for one generation to pass on to the next. I wanted it to be emblematic of the whole city, so I had a local steelworks cut the bars. I finished them by hand, and a Sheffield engraver added winners' names in a typeface from Stephenson & Blake, the city's foundry and once the world's largest maker of metal type.

![A Steel Core trophy stamped MADE IN SHEFFIELD ENGLAND](render.png)

### The impact

Creative media company It's Nice That reported the Grand Jury winner left "with the coveted award stuffed into his jacket pocket," which was indeed nice, as I had designed the trophies to be easily carried. DocFest contracted me to produce trophies 3 years running, 33 awards in total, before the COVID-19 pandemic cancelled the awards ceremonies.

![Sheffield DocFest's 2017 award winners in a group, holding their steel trophies](winners-2017.jpg){16:9}

### The challenge

All artists hope their work will sell one day. In 2016, I needed mine to sell within weeks. I had been offered a coveted place on a master's at the Royal College of Art London, starting that September, but I had no way to pay for it, as student loans for master's are limited.

![](crowdfunding.mp4 "My crowdfunding video (3 mins)")

### What I did

I went home to Sheffield and gave myself 6 weeks to make and sell out a solo show, alongside a crowdfunding campaign on Indiegogo. I was into socially engaged art, so I recruited strangers in the streets, asking them about Sheffield's past, present, and future, and turning their responses into original artworks.

![A Sheffield road sign reading Sheffield You Were History, beside 4 faces of Sheffielders from the film](sheffielders.png){whole}

Because the show simply *had* to sell out, I made many pieces in batches, reminiscent of the city's factories, with a range of prices starting at £20 so everybody could take something home. I organised everything myself, from research to production to hanging the show, with backing from 4 local businesses.

![4 views of the show: word mirrors, bright portraits, a row of framed drawings and small casts of Sheffield's outline](artworks.png)

### The impact

The pricing strategy and engagement with the Sheffield community paid off. In 6 weeks, I made 63 artworks. 30 people came to the opening night, and the show sold £3,000 of work. The Indiegogo campaign raised £1,800 more, and together that money helped me accept the offer from the Royal College of Art, which totally changed my life! :)

![Close-up of a mirror cut with words Sheffielders chose, like nostalgia, global and aspiring](mirror.png)

I can develop creative solutions by researching in the field, design for participation, and connect ideas from different worlds. I'd love to be directing these skills towards problems and solutions in the future of education, like:
